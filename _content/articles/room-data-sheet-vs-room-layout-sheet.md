Two documents describe the same room, yet they answer different questions. One says what the room must do and contain. The other shows where everything goes. Mixing them up is a quiet source of rework on healthcare projects.

## Two documents, two questions

Health New Zealand's guidance on Room Data Sheets (RDS) gives their purpose as establishing the project brief at a room-by-room level of detail, and as the foundation information for the Room Layout Sheets. Its guidance on Room Layout Sheets (RLS) describes them as a graphical representation of the brief requirements detailed in the associated RDS.

In short:

- **RDS:** the written brief for one room. What it is for, how big, and what it needs.
- **RLS:** the drawn answer to that brief. How the room is arranged.

![Infographic: the RDS is the written brief and the RLS is the drawn response. Changes flow from the brief to the layout](images/room-data-sheet-vs-room-layout-sheet-compare.webp)

## What an RDS holds

An example from the NHS Property Services standard design material shows a typical sheet. Each room has its own sheet, keyed by a room code, with activities, planning relationships, space data such as area and ceiling height, environmental data and design character. A "Schedule of Components by Room" lists the items the room contains, by code, group and quantity. That schedule is, in practice, the room's equipment list.

NHSScotland's Repeatable Rooms report follows the same idea. It publishes room data sheets for standardised rooms as an appendix, and notes that additional items may be added project by project.

Health New Zealand adds two process points. Sheets are built from the Australasian Health Facility Guidelines (AusHFG) standard component RDS, and any deviation from the standard is recorded with its date, amendment and reason. Briefed ceiling heights are captured in the project RDS.

## What an RLS shows

According to Health New Zealand, a Room Layout Sheet provides the proposed layout as a dimensioned plan, wall elevations and a 3D room layout. It also covers materials, engineering services, and equipment placement and labelling. NHSScotland publishes 1:50 room layouts of its repeatable rooms in a separate appendix, which is the same kind of drawing under a different name.

For an equipment planner, this is where a line on a list becomes a position on a wall: a unit's location, clearances, and the services it connects to.

## How they stay in step

Health New Zealand states that changes to the RLS are driven by annotated changes to the briefing documents, meaning the RDS. The direction matters. If the brief changes, the layout follows, and the change is recorded. If someone moves an item on the layout and the RDS is not updated, the two documents now disagree.

Both documents are also tied to project stages. Health New Zealand expects project RDS and RLS sets at Preliminary Design, with client endorsement on each design stage set. A practical routine for teams:

1. Agree the room code system first, and use it on every sheet and list.
2. Start each project RDS from the relevant standard sheet and log every deviation.
3. Update the RDS before, or together with, the RLS, never after.
4. Check that the equipment in the RLS matches the room's schedule of components, item by item.
5. Re-issue both documents together at each design stage.

## Where the model comes in

In a Revit workflow, the same logic applies. Room parameters and equipment families can carry the data that an RDS needs, and views of the room can serve as layouts. The principle does not change: one source for the brief, drawings that follow it.

## Takeaway

The RDS is the brief, the RLS is the response, and the room's equipment list connects them. Keep one direction of change, from brief to layout, and the two documents will agree when the room is built.
